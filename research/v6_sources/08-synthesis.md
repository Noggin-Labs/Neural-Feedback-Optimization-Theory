# NFOT v6: Cross-Domain Literature Synthesis

## Synthesis

Across the supplied evidence, **feedback timing is consequential but not governed by a single universal optimum**. Its effects depend on the level of the system being timed, the outcome being optimized, the task, and the measurement definition.

At the **dataset and event-semantics level**, bigP3BCI provides a usable vocabulary for distinguishing pre-run, pre-trial, trial, and post-trial phases; stimulus on/off and target/non-target events; character presentation and current target; and predicted selections versus displayed feedback. The documentation explicitly positions these fields for P300 ERP and error-related-potential analyses. This makes NFOT-style timing analyses possible only when event labels are interpreted according to the official data dictionary rather than inferred from filenames or nominal protocol descriptions.

At the **BCI performance level**, more frequent feedback sometimes improved training-stage P300-speller classification: the clearest effect occurred at four flashes per row/column, with a trend at six, but no significant differences at eight or ten and no significant group-by-flash-count interaction. In restorative BCI evidence, a single stroke case improved 36% on ARAT after ten sessions using 16–96 ms proprioceptive update intervals, while an offline classifier study reported 67% accuracy with a 750-ms window and 32-ms updates. These findings support timing as a design variable, not a demonstrated optimal interval: the studies differ in paradigm, outcome, causal exposure, and participant population.

The **synaptic literature** supplies a mechanistic rationale for why delayed signals can remain effective. Ex-vivo cortical, hippocampal, and corticostriatal studies support a three-factor pattern: local activity establishes a transient eligibility state, and a later monoaminergic signal gates potentiation, depression, or structural change. The strongest direct silent-trace result showed dopamine-dependent potentiation when dopamine arrived about two seconds after a tested corticostriatal pattern, with tested offsets of −2, 0, and +4 seconds failing under that protocol. Other work found narrower or different windows, including approximately 0.3–2 seconds for D1-neuron spine enlargement and immediate-to-minutes-scale effects in hippocampal plasticity. Together, these results show that delayed reinforcement signals can interact with prior activity, but they do not define a universal neural timing kernel or establish behavioral causality.

In **human neurofeedback and educational neuroadaptive systems**, latency also matters, but evidence remains outcome-specific. A controlled parietal-alpha experiment found larger sustained alpha changes with immediate feedback than with 250- or 500-ms delays, indicating that feedback can be most effective while the relevant neural state is ongoing. By contrast, AttentivU used five-second EEG windows, a 15-second engagement estimate, and a 15-second persistence trigger and reported improved engagement and immediate comprehension. NeuroChat averaged one-second EEG epochs over a 15-second sliding window updated each second; engagement increased, but short-term quiz and essay outcomes did not significantly improve. Thus, educational systems currently support feasible adaptive timing architectures, not a validated educational latency rule or long-term learning benefit attributable specifically to timing.

The **learning and causal-feedback literature** cautions against equating faster feedback with better learning. Delayed feedback improved delayed cued recall, later engineering-course exam performance on novel problems, or later short-answer performance in several experiments. However, other experiments found no timing difference, and one delayed-feedback benefit appeared for short-answer but not multiple-choice outcomes. Effects also depended on feedback viewing, spacing and retention intervals, prior response correctness/confidence, task context, and downstream test format. The bounded conclusion is that timing can causally alter later learning or transfer under some conditions; neither immediate nor delayed feedback is universally superior.

For **NFOT v6 analysis and evaluation**, repeated observations should be modeled with methods that represent within-participant dependence and incomplete longitudinal records, such as mixed-effects approaches where appropriate. Predictive validation must match the intended deployment target: participant-wise grouped splits are needed when estimating generalization to unseen participants, while future-measurement splits may be more appropriate for within-participant prognosis. GroupKFold prevents the same supplied group from appearing in training and test folds, but requires enough groups, may yield imbalanced folds, and is only as valid as the group definition. Model selection and preprocessing must remain inside training folds; nested validation addresses selection bias but does not by itself prevent participant leakage.

Finally, **physical timing must be separated from software timing**. Independent photodetector and photodiode studies found configuration-dependent discrepancies between nominal intervals, software/TTL triggers, and measured display transitions. Tested packages often had sub-millisecond trial-to-trial precision, yet visual onset commonly included roughly four milliseconds of hardware- and scan-position-dependent lag. Browser timing improved in many tested Windows cases with a continuously running `requestAnimationFrame` loop but remained OS/browser dependent, with Linux comparatively variable. Psychtoolbox timestamps are high-precision estimates whose reliability depends on backend, driver, OS, and hardware. When NFOT claims concern actual stimulus onset, external measurement with an explicit luminance/onset criterion is the defensible reference; no universal software-to-photon conversion is supported.

## Cross-domain implications

1. **Define the timing target before optimizing it.** Possible targets include classifier update, delivered sensory feedback, neural-state alignment, synaptic eligibility, display transition, later recall, or unseen-participant prediction. These are not interchangeable.
2. **Treat timing as multiscale and conditional.** Millisecond-to-second neural windows, 15-second adaptive estimates, flash-count-dependent P300 decisions, and week-delayed educational feedback address different mechanisms and outcomes.
3. **Separate nominal, recorded, and physical events.** NFOT analyses should preserve the distinction between protocol labels, software/API timestamps, externally measured display onset, and the participant’s received feedback.
4. **Use causal language narrowly.** Timing effects are strongest when timing is experimentally manipulated and the downstream outcome is measured; offline classification, single-case intervention, ex-vivo physiology, and prototype associations support hypotheses rather than universal prescriptions.
5. **Make participant structure part of validation.** Repeated records can inflate performance estimates if participant identity crosses splits. The split strategy should reflect whether NFOT v6 is intended to generalize to new participants or forecast future observations within a participant.

## Evidence boundaries

- The supplied evidence does **not** establish a universal optimal feedback latency, update interval, flash count, or educational timing rule.
- bigP3BCI is heterogeneous across 20 study IDs, paradigms, grids, sessions, and participant groups; some files and demographic data are missing, and labels largely derive from BCI2000 definitions rather than independent re-annotation.
- BCI timing findings are paradigm- and pipeline-dependent; the stroke result is single-case, and the 32-ms classifier result is offline rather than evidence about delivered sensory feedback.
- Eligibility-trace findings are predominantly acute slice studies using imposed stimulation, exogenous or uncaged monoamines, and restricted circuits/cell types; molecular mechanisms and behavioral consequences remain unresolved.
- Educational neuroadaptive evidence lacks randomized latency comparisons and delayed retention outcomes; engagement and immediate comprehension are not equivalent to durable learning.
- Delayed-feedback learning benefits are conditional and sometimes absent; transfer depends on task, test format, interval structure, and context.
- General statistical guidance does not constitute an NFOT repository implementation claim; correct validation depends on the true dependency structure and deployment target.
- Software timestamps and nominal stimulus commands are not physical display onset. Hardware, scan-out, pixel response, drivers, browsers, operating systems, and photodetector thresholds can add lag or uncertainty.
- The synthesis uses only the structured findings supplied for notes 01–07 and makes no independent claims about the NFOT repository beyond the requested cross-domain interpretation.
